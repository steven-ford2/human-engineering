# Stick and inceptor-control workload papers: primary-source audit

Research check: 2026-10-15. The user's pasted search-result list is an **intake lead**, not evidence that each paper measured physical stick deflection or validated it as workload. This note checks selected high-value records against original papers, agency reports, author-hosted reports, or original publisher abstracts. It does not claim an exhaustive search of studies using these measures. Page numbers below are printed pages unless prefixed “PDF page.”

## Full texts inspected

### Hebbar and Pashilkar (2016) — direct stick-derived PIW and spectral analysis

**Identity and access.** Archana Hebbar and Abhay A. Pashilkar, “Application of Quantitative Measures for Analysing Aircraft Handling Qualities,” *Defence Science Journal* 66(1), 3–10 (2016), [doi:10.14429/dsj.66.9196](https://doi.org/10.14429/dsj.66.9196). The [original DRDO journal page](https://publicationsdrdo.in/index.php/dsj/article/view/9196) and its [official full-text PDF](https://publicationsdrdo.in/index.php/dsj/article/download/9196/5442) were retrieved on a second attempt. A [separate hosting copy](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/dd27/c23f9d0950d6ca2919d2233c2bf2bfe1a827.pdf) is not another study.

- **What was done:** Three pilots flew six flight sorties containing 19 pitch/roll tracking segments in two configurations of an unnamed high-performance fighter; a moving HUD target was tracked with both axes. The paper also analyzes three pitch- and two roll-tracking simulator segments for comparison. See pp. 3–4, §§1–2.1 and Table 1. The article does **not** identify the aircraft model, so do not assign one.
- **Operationalization:** PIW is a two-dimensional plot of RMS rate of stick-position change (“aggressiveness,” reported in mm/s) against percentage of time the stick input changes beyond a noise threshold (“duty cycle”). The threshold is 0.5% of total inceptor displacement range per time increment. The authors additionally compute PSD of stick-input time series, autocorrelation of successive commands, and flight-path tracking metrics. See pp. 4–6, §2.2, Eqs. for aggressiveness/duty cycle, Figs. 2–3. Their reported PIW uses physical deflection samples, not a questionnaire score.
- **Interpretation and limits:** The authors found more active/faster pitch than roll inputs in the dual-axis tracking data, and describe changed pitch-control activity between configurations; they interpret these in context of task difficulty and tracking accuracy (pp. 6–8, §§3–6). This is a small, context-specific comparison, not a psychometric validation or universal workload threshold. The study's pitch and roll tracking commands had markedly different ranges (±3° vs ±60°, p. 6), so raw axis comparisons are task-confounded. It does not establish that larger stick motion always means greater cognitive workload.

### Hanson et al. (2014) — *different* aggressiveness and duty-cycle definitions

**Identity and access.** Curt Hanson, Jacob Schaefer, John J. Burken, David Larson, and Marcus Johnson, “Complexity and Pilot Workload Metrics for the Evaluation of Adaptive Flight Controls on a Full Scale Piloted Aircraft,” NASA/TM–2014–216640 (Feb. 2014), [NASA record](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20140005730), [full PDF](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140005730/downloads/20140005730.pdf).

- **What was done:** Two test pilots performed 2-g air-to-air tracking maneuvers in NASA's modified F-18 Full-Scale Advanced Systems Testbed. The report compares a nonadaptive controller (with/without simulated reduced-pitch-damping failure) and three adaptive variants under the simulated failure (pp. 2, 11–12, Fig. 5). The F-15 IFCS controller is a complexity comparator; do not describe the F-15 as the aircraft generating the workload data in Fig. 5.
- **Operationalization:** Aggressiveness is the maneuver-time mean of absolute unbiased pitch and roll inceptor inputs normalized to each axis's total allowable deflection range. A forward/backward low-pass filter estimates and removes slow, nonzero bias before the axes are combined. “Duty cycle” here counts control-input peaks/reversals **per second** across both axes when a reversal exceeds 0.5% of total displacement range. See p. 11, §“Aggressiveness and Duty Cycle Metrics,” Eq. (2), Fig. 4. The authors explicitly say this reversals-per-second metric differs from Shepherd's nonsteady-compensation percentage; do **not** equate it with Hebbar's or Niewind's percentage-of-time-active duty cycle (p. 12).
- **Interpretation and limits:** In these data, simulated failure increased the input metrics and adaptation reduced them, with different patterns for the two pilots (pp. 11–12, Fig. 5; p. 14, Summary). The paper treats these as context-dependent indicators to aid handling-qualities interpretation, not as stand-alone validated mental-workload measurement. It reports that the earlier nonsteady-compensation method poorly discriminated the controllers because inputs varied almost continuously (p. 12).

### Niewind (2012, IB 111-2012/74) — two-dimensional PIW and proposed alternatives

**Identity and access.** Ina Niewind, *Pilot Gain and the Workload Buildup Flight Test Technique: A Closer Investigation of Pilot Inceptor Workload*, DLR-IB 111-2012/74 (28 Oct. 2012), [DLR record](https://elib.dlr.de/88208/), [full PDF](https://elib.dlr.de/88208/1/IB%20111-2012-74%20Niewind.pdf). This is a DLR institute report, not the distinct 2011 CEAS article.

- **What was done:** The report analyzes a fixed-base simulator study with 24 military pilots instructed to apply low, normal, and high gain during sum-of-sines tracking and variants of a workload-buildup boundary task (overview, p. I; §§3.1.5–3.1.7, pp. 16–18). It also compares **published/open-source** BAT DART NF-16D flight information; the DLR report did not conduct those F-16 flights. Table 3-7 (p. 20) explicitly contrasts the HUD-equipped flight dataset with the monitor-based simulator dataset, including task frequency and data-window differences.
- **Operationalization:** The baseline PIW is RMS stick speed (“aggressiveness”) versus percentage of time the stick moves (“duty cycle”), a two-dimensional *pilot gain/control-activity* representation (overview, p. I; §1.3, pp. 3–4). Chapter 5 (pp. 36–42) explores **alternatives**, including RMS stick deflection versus RMS stick speed, deflection/duty cycle, speed×deflection/duty cycle, and acceleration/duty cycle. These alternatives must not be reported as all equivalent formulations of the original PIW. The report also investigates proposed one-dimensional reductions of PIW in Chapters 2 and 4.
- **Interpretation and limits:** The low- and high-gain simulator runs separated in PIW plots, supporting PIW as a representation of **pilot gain under this tracking task** (§6.2, p. 43). Niewind cautions that high-gain points differ by technique and task, that comparisons with BAT DART have many differing factors, and that more studies are needed before generalizing the shared trend (§6.2, p. 43; Table 3-7). A one-dimensional score loses the aggressiveness-versus-duty-cycle slope visible in the two-dimensional plot (§4.4, p. 35). RMS stick speed and deflection were related in this dataset but their relationship depends on individual control strategy, task, and aircraft frequency response (§5.1, p. 36). These are not questionnaire reliability coefficients or construct-validity proof for mental workload.

### Niewind (2012, IB 111-2012/69) — pilot-gain range and person-specificity

**Identity and access.** Ina Niewind, *Pilot Gain and the Workload Buildup Flight Test Technique: About the Influence of Natural Pilot Gain on the Achievable Pilot Gain Range*, DLR-IB 111-2012/69 (2012), [full PDF](https://elib.dlr.de/88213/1/IB%20111-2012-69%20Niewind.pdf). It uses data from 12 experimental test pilots and 12 operational pilots intentionally varying gain in a simulator tracking task (Summary/Outlook, p. 38; study design, Ch. 2).

- **Measures analyzed:** Mean and RMS stick speed, percentages of high stick speeds, stick-acceleration variants, and stick-input PSD area/ratios are compared as pilot-gain measures (Ch. 4, pp. 16–35). This is not a new fighter flight dataset.
- **Interpretation and limits:** Absolute achievable gain range tended to increase with natural gain, while relative range tended to decrease. The report emphasizes considerable scatter and poor regression fit, explicitly declining individual-pilot prediction from these population trends (Summary/Outlook, p. 38). This directly cautions against fixed, person-independent “high workload” thresholds derived from inceptor activity.

### Mayer and Cox (2003) — **normalized control command**, not interchangeable physical deflection

**Identity and access.** Jann Mayer and Timothy H. Cox, *Evaluation of Two Unique Side Stick Controllers in a Fixed-Base Flight Simulator*, NASA/TM–2003–212042 (Dec. 2003), [NASA record](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20040013308), [full PDF](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20040013308/downloads/20040013308.pdf).

- **What was done:** Seven research pilots compared two large-displacement sidesticks with a standard center stick in a fixed-base F-18 simulator using fighter maneuvers, with Cooper–Harper and PIO ratings and comments (p. 45, Conclusions). The paper examines target acquisition, tracking, and offset landing, among other tasks (task descriptions, pp. 7–10).
- **Operationalization:** The authors called the standard deviation of the **flight-control command time history** an estimate of “workload” (average command amplitude) and computed command rate as total command travel divided by maneuver duration, separately by axis. Critically, those calculations used the command sent to the flight-control algorithms, **not raw stick position**, to normalize the three differently moving sticks. Physical side-stick deflections were significantly smaller than center-stick deflections, while commands to the system could be larger (p. 23, §“Pilot Aggressiveness”). Time histories and control commands were compared with pilot ratings and comments (pp. 23–26).
- **Interpretation and limits:** The ratings and time histories showed preference depended on pilot control style and mechanical damping/breakout characteristics (pp. 23–30, 45–46). Do not cite this as a direct test of “physical stick deflection = workload,” nor treat the report's locally named command-SD “workload” as cognitive-workload validity evidence.

## Primary publisher abstracts/records only — do not infer full methods or coefficients

### Babu (2022) — PIW in simulator and jet-trainer flight

M. Dilli Babu, “Investigation of Pilot Inceptor Workload and Workload Buildup Technique Through Simulator and In-Flight Studies,” *The International Journal of Aerospace Psychology* 32(2–3), 65–94 (2022), published online 26 Jul. 2021, [doi:10.1080/24721840.2021.1937173](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/24721840.2021.1937173). The **publisher abstract** confirms two fixed-base variable-stability simulator studies with military test pilots and an in-flight advanced-jet-trainer air-to-ground tracking study. It names flying qualities, secondary task, and closing-boundary effects as independent variables and reports statistically significant relationships of PIW with conditions. The full article's exact PIW calculation, sample sizes, tests, and effect estimates were not verified here; do not copy details from search snippets or preprints into an appraised record as if they were the published article.

### Bachelder (2020) — control activity alone is insufficient

Edward N. Bachelder, “SCOPE – Pilot Workload Estimation Using Control Response: Theoretical Development and Practical Demonstration,” AIAA SciTech 2020, [doi:10.2514/6.2020-2267](https://doi.org/10.2514/6.2020-2267); [author university repository and original abstract](https://scholarworks.sjsu.edu/faculty_rsca/1740/). The abstract says SCOPE uses error-rate PSD shaped by pilot compensation, and variants using vehicle dynamics/output when tracking error is unavailable. It expressly warns that stick motion alone is not a good workload estimator and compensation must be considered; an experimental tracking-task comparison with Bedford ratings is described. The full conference paper, sample/task details, and reported correlation calculation were not independently checked, so the abstract's numerical correlation is **not promoted here as general validity evidence**.

## Cross-study curation rules

1. **Name the signal:** raw stick displacement, stick speed, command sent to flight controls, reversal frequency, and PSD are not interchangeable. Mayer's normalized command is a particularly important exception.
2. **Name the operational definition:** “PIW,” “aggressiveness,” and “duty cycle” vary by source. Hanson 2014's reversal count per second is not Hebbar/Niewind's percent time moving.
3. **Name the intended interpretation:** these papers are largely about pilot gain, control effort/activity, handling qualities, and task-specific workload; none of the sources checked establishes a universal cognitive-workload proxy or acceptance threshold for a new test bed.
4. **Keep dataset families separate:** Niewind 2012 reports reuse DLR simulator data and external BAT DART flight information; Hebbar 2016 uses flight plus comparable simulator segments; do not inflate independent-study counts.
5. **Match evidence to test-bed task:** pair inceptor activity with tracking error/performance and an independent subjective rating, record aircraft dynamics and controller settings, and predefine signal scaling/noise thresholds. This is a synthesis recommendation from the checked papers, **not** a standardized protocol or MIL-STD compliance rule.
